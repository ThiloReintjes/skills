# Crisp writing

Write so the intended reader can grasp the point, follow the reasoning, judge the evidence, and know what follows. Deliver the requested piece. Keep the planning and editorial checks internal unless the user asks to see them.

## First principles

- **Attention is limited.** Give each passage a reader's question to answer. Its position and length should reflect how much the answer matters to this reader.
- **Understanding requires connections.** Explain the link between a fact, its consequence, and a proposed action when the reader would otherwise have to infer it. A short explanation can save more effort than a shorter document.
- **Trust depends on fidelity.** The force of a claim must match its evidence. Preserve uncertainty, attribution, scope, and meaningful disagreement through every edit.
- **Voice carries meaning.** Preserve the writer's stance and natural cadence. Specific language and connected prose should carry the argument; formatting should make it easier to navigate.

Resolve trade-offs in this order: fidelity to facts and intent, usefulness to the reader, ease of reading, brevity.

## 1. Establish the brief

Read the full draft or supplied material. Identify the reader, the question they need answered, the desired outcome, and the medium. Note constraints such as length, required sections, tone, sources, and wording to preserve. For an edit, notice the writer's distinctive vocabulary, cadence, humor, bluntness, and uncertainty.

Infer routine choices from context. Ask a concise question only when the missing answer would materially change the message or require inventing a consequential fact. Continue work that does not depend on it. With sparse evidence, narrow the claim or make a provisional draft with clearly marked gaps.

Match the intervention to the request:

- **Draft from notes:** synthesize an argument and write the complete piece.
- **Revise:** repair structure when it obstructs understanding; preserve strong passages. A request to shorten or polish does not authorize changing the writer's position.
- **Copyedit:** preserve the argument and order; flag a substantive problem separately when needed.
- **Critique only:** quote the consequential weaknesses, explain their effect on this reader, and suggest concrete fixes. Return findings without a full rewrite or claims about AI authorship.

For slides, decks, or presentation storylines, read [Presentation guidance](references/presentations.md) before outlining. This skill governs content; use the appropriate artifact tools when the user requests a file.

**Ready when:** you can state internally, “This reader needs to understand or decide X, given Y.”

## 2. Establish what can be said

Form a provisional answer to the reader's question. For a recommendation, identify the decision criteria and strongest realistic alternative, including the current approach where relevant. Look for supplied evidence that could overturn the answer.

Keep these distinctions clear while working:

| Material | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Supported fact | Preserve its source, population, timeframe, and relevant comparison. |
| Calculation | Check the arithmetic, units, denominator, and comparability of the inputs. |
| Interpretation | Make the reasoning visible and match causal language to the evidence. |
| Proposal or forecast | Identify it as proposed or expected; state assumptions that affect the choice. |
| Unknown or conflicting evidence | Resolve it from available sources or retain the uncertainty where it affects the claim. |

Do not invent facts, citations, quotes, examples presented as real, or commitments to make prose more concrete. Propose actions within the task's scope; distinguish suggested owners, dates, budgets, and targets from agreed ones. If a claim needs new research, use the available sources within the user's scope or flag the gap. A polished sentence cannot repair missing evidence.

**Ready when:** every claim central to the message has support, a defensible inference, or an explicit qualification; material contrary evidence is accounted for.

## 3. Build the argument

Use the Pyramid Principle to put the answer early and group the reasoning beneath it. A useful lead states a conclusion; a label such as “There are several opportunities” leaves the synthesis to the reader.

Choose supporting points that answer the next questions this reader will ask. Test the structure in both directions:

- From the conclusion downward: does each point explain why the conclusion holds, how it works, or what follows?
- From the evidence upward: do the points actually justify the conclusion, including its scale and certainty?

Use MECE to check logical groupings for gaps and double counting. Keep sibling points at a comparable level and order them by relevance, cause, sequence, or another clear relationship. Use as many reasons as the argument needs. An investigation's issue tree need not become the document's outline.

Use SCQA to establish the reader's question when context is missing: shared situation, consequential change, question, answer. Keep setup proportional to the reader's familiarity. An explanation may need a definition or example first; a sensitive message may need a brief acknowledgment. Earn any delay before the main point.

Apply What / So What / Now What to findings: what happened, why it affects the reader, and what follows. Include only the links the reader needs; these need not become section labels. An explanation can end in understanding without manufacturing an action request.

**Ready when:** the outline answers the reader's question, each section earns its place, and the decisive reasons support the conclusion without hidden leaps.

## 4. Write for continuous reading

Write connected prose in which each sentence develops the previous one. Give a paragraph one main job while allowing examples, qualifications, and transitions to support it.

- **Put meaning in the nouns and verbs.** Name the actor, action, object, and consequence when known. Replace abstract activity with what someone did or will do. Keep necessary technical terms and explain unfamiliar ones at first use.
- **Prefer specific, defensible language.** Use a supplied number, comparison, mechanism, or example when it clarifies the point. Keep useful detail during compression. Qualitative precision is enough when no number is supported.
- **State the subject directly.** Replace generic openers, staged revelations, rhetorical questions, and formulaic “not X, but Y” framing with the actual claim. Preserve comparisons and distinctions that the reasoning needs.
- **Let evidence carry emphasis.** Replace promotional adjectives, declarations of importance, and trailing claims about what a fact “highlights” with its supported consequence. Keep an explanation that supplies a missing logical link.
- **Use stable terminology.** Repeat the right word instead of cycling through synonyms. Make pronoun references unambiguous.
- **Preserve natural cadence.** Mix sentence lengths as the reasoning requires. Keep a clear long sentence or characteristic fragment. Join stacked fragments when they make the reader reconstruct relationships. Use punctuation for syntax rather than drama.
- **Keep honest voice.** Preserve meaningful hedges, admissions, humor, and strong opinions. Retain appropriate warmth. In a new draft, use a calm, direct tone without invented personality, anecdotes, or certainty.
- **Make structure carry weight.** Use headings for navigation, lists for parallel items or steps, and tables for comparisons. A short piece may need none. Prefer headings that reveal the conclusion when useful; keep topic labels where they better support lookup. End with the last useful consequence or next step.

**Ready when:** the piece reads naturally from start to finish and preserves the reasoning, specificity, and voice the reader needs.

## 5. Cut and verify

Review the actual draft, not your intention. Fix failures before delivery. Stop when these checks pass; further polishing should solve an identifiable problem.

| Check | Observable condition |
|---|---|
| Opening | The opening supplies the answer or the minimum context needed to understand it. |
| Skim | The lead, headings where present, and closing let this reader reconstruct the main point and any request. |
| Skeptic | The recommendation addresses the strongest material objection or trade-off. Facts, estimates, and assumptions remain distinguishable. |
| Fidelity | Every material number, qualifier, source, and commitment survives accurately. No useful uncertainty became confidence. |
| Deletion | Each passage adds meaning, evidence, orientation, necessary tone, or a useful connection. Remove passages whose deletion loses none of these. |
| Specificity | A sentence that could fit an unrelated subject earns its place through a necessary definition or connection; otherwise replace it with supported substance or cut it. |
| Read-aloud | The draft sounds natural when spoken to this reader. Tangled clauses, repeated sentence shapes, decorative emphasis, and forced punch lines are resolved. |
| Action | Where action is needed, the reader can identify the requested decision or next step. Include owner, timing, resources, and success criteria only as relevant and supported or explicitly proposed. |
| Fit | The piece satisfies the requested medium, length, structure, and degree of editing. |

For a short note, apply these checks mentally. For a long document, inspect the argument and the prose in separate passes. Brevity succeeds when the reader does less work while retaining what matters.

## Delivery

Return the requested document or artifact first. Show assumptions or unresolved evidence only when the user needs them to use the result. After a substantive revision, briefly explain material changes if useful; omit commentary when the user requests only the text. For critique-only work, return the findings defined in step 1.
